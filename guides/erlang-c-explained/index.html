Erlang C explained: a worked call-centre staffing example

Erlang C is the standard formula contact centres use to answer one question: how many agents do we need to answer a given share of calls within a target time? Here is how it works in plain English, with a full example you can check in the free calculator.

The inputs

  • Call volume for an interval, for example 150 calls between 11:00 and 12:00.
  • Average handle time (AHT): talk time plus hold plus after-call work, for example 300 seconds.
  • Service-level target: for example 80% of calls answered within 20 seconds ("80/20").
  • Shrinkage: the share of paid time agents are not available for calls (breaks, meetings, training, absence), often 25-35%.

Step 1: traffic intensity

Traffic intensity, measured in Erlangs, is how many agents would be busy if calls arrived perfectly evenly:

Traffic = calls × AHT ÷ interval length = 150 × 300 ÷ 3,600 = 12.5 Erlangs.

So you need more than 12.5 agents just to keep up. With exactly 13, queues grow quickly whenever calls bunch together, which they always do.

Step 2: probability a call waits

Erlang C calculates the chance that an arriving call has to wait, given the traffic and a number of agents. With more agents, the chance falls sharply. The formula assumes calls arrive randomly, callers wait in a queue rather than hanging up, and every agent can take every call.

Step 3: service level

From the waiting probability, Erlang C gives the share of calls answered within your target time. You add agents one at a time until you reach the target. In this example:

AgentsService level (20 s)Occupancy
14about 47%89%
15about 66%83%
16about 79%78%
17about 88%74%

So 17 agents on the phones meets 80/20 for this hour (16 falls just short at 79%). Notice how one agent changes service level by 10-20 points. That is why under-staffing by "just one or two" hurts so much.

Step 4: add shrinkage

If 30% of paid time is lost to breaks, meetings and absence, you must schedule more people than you need on the phones:

Scheduled = on the phones ÷ (1 − shrinkage) = 17 ÷ 0.70 = 24.3, so 25 agents (always round up).

Where Erlang C goes wrong

  • Abandoned calls: real callers hang up. Erlang C ignores this, so it slightly overstates staff needed when abandonment is high.
  • Multi-skill queues and chat or email: agents handling several channels need simulation or more advanced models.
  • Bad forecasts: the formula is only as good as your call forecast. Forecast each 15- or 30-minute interval from your own history, including day-of-week and seasonal patterns.
