Why these checks matter

Most strategies that look great in a backtest fail live for a handful of reasons: costs set too low, signals that accidentally use information from the future, too few trades, and too many parameters tuned to the past. A good backtest is a tool for rejecting ideas cheaply, not for proving them. For the full explanation, read why your EA works in backtest but fails live.

How the score works

The 12 checks add up to 100 points. The biggest weights go to the mistakes that most often turn a losing strategy into a winning backtest: a realistic spread (12 points), signals that only use fully closed candles (12), at least 200 trades (10) and an out-of-sample test (10). 80 or more is a strong backtest, 50 to 79 has red flags to fix first, and under 50 means the result may be mostly costs, luck or look-ahead bias.

Worked example: one unchecked box

In my own research, a strategy that looked validated returned +0.45R per trade. Its entry read candles that had not closed yet: the "signals only use fully closed candles" check. After fixing that one bug, the same strategy returned −0.16R per trade. One unchecked box was the difference between a winning and a losing system.

Questions

How many trades does a backtest need?

This checklist asks for at least 200. Under about 100-200 trades, a good result can easily be luck.

What is out-of-sample testing?

Tuning the strategy on one part of the data and testing it once on data it never saw, for example the last 25-30% of the history.

What is look-ahead bias?

Using information that was not available when the trade would have been placed, such as the close of a candle that has not finished. It makes backtests look far better than live trading.

Does a high score mean the strategy will make money?

No. A high score means the backtest is more trustworthy, not that the strategy is profitable. Forward-test on demo before trading live.
